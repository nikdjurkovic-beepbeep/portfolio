Internal Linking at Scale: How Website Architecture Influences Discovery and Rankings

On a large site, internal linking is architecture, not a writing habit. Templates decide which pages Google finds, how far they sit from the home page and how much importance they receive. Controlled tests show the gains are real, and uneven.

Key takeaways

  • Internal links do two jobs. They let Google discover URLs, and they tell Google which pages the site treats as important. A sitemap does the first job, not the second.
  • PageRank is still part of Google’s system. Notes of a 2025 call with a Google engineer, published as an exhibit in the US antitrust case, describe it as “a single signal relating to distance from a known good source”, used as an input to Google’s quality score.
  • On large sites, templates set click depth. In a worked example, the same 10,000 products sit four clicks from the home page or up to 501, depending only on pagination and category structure.
  • Published split tests show gains of 7% to 25% when templates added links to pages that had few, and no clear effect where the linked pages were likely strong already.
  • Internal links redistribute importance; they do not create it. Every link added to a template shares that page’s value with every other link on it.

Two jobs of an internal link

Internal linking is often treated as a writing habit: add a few contextual links to each new article. On a site with hundreds of thousands of URLs, that habit barely registers. The links that matter are generated by templates: navigation, category listings, pagination, breadcrumbs, filters and related-item modules. They decide which pages Google finds and how much weight those pages carry.

Google’s documentation names both jobs in one sentence: “Google uses links as a signal when determining the relevancy of pages and to find new pages to crawl.” [1] The first job is discovery. Googlebot extracts URLs from pages it has already fetched and adds them to its queue. The second is weighting. The links pointing to a page, and the words on them, tell Google how important the page is and what it covers.

Discovery has a narrow technical condition. “Generally, Google can only crawl your link if it’s an <a> HTML element” with an href attribute, and “most links in other formats won’t be parsed and extracted by Google’s crawlers.” [1] A clickable span or an onclick handler can work for users and stay invisible to the crawler. The JavaScript version of the problem is covered in what Google’s renderer does not do.

Google’s rule for internal links is short: “Every page you care about should have a link from at least one other page on your site.” [1] Its sitemap documentation says the same from the other side. A site may not need a sitemap if it “is comprehensively linked internally”, which means that Googlebot “can find all the important pages on your site by following links starting from the home page.” Sitemaps help large and new sites, but a sitemap “doesn’t guarantee that all the items in your sitemap will be crawled and indexed.” [2]

A sitemap covers discovery. It does not cover importance. In 2021 a site owner whose sitemap URLs were only about 10% indexed asked how to tell Google which pages mattered, since Google ignores the priority field in sitemaps. John Mueller of Google answered: “To give a sense of importance within your website you’d need to do that more with regards to internal linking.” [3] A year later he was asked whether breadcrumb structured data made internal links less necessary. He called internal linking “super critical for SEO” and “one of the biggest things that you can do on a website to kind of guide Google”, and said of the URLs in structured data: “we don’t use those URLs in the same way as we would use normal internal links on a page.” [4] Markup describes a page for the features that read it, as the schema markup study shows. It does not replace the site’s links.

How links carry importance

The weighting job goes back to PageRank. In the 1998 paper that described Google, Sergey Brin and Larry Page modelled a “random surfer” who keeps clicking on links and “eventually gets bored and starts on another random page”. A page’s PageRank is the probability that this surfer visits it. [5] Each page passes on its score through a damping factor, usually set to 0.85, divided by C(A), “the number of links going out of page A”. The paper also attached anchor text to the target page: “we associate it with the page the link points to.” [5]

Two properties of that formula explain most of what follows. Importance flows from pages that already have it, and every extra link on a page divides it further. In the original formula, a listing page with 60 links passes about 1.4% of its score through each one. Two such steps leave about 0.02%. Google’s current system is not the 1998 formula, but the arithmetic shows why depth and link counts matter at all.

Google has never published how PageRank works today. It has confirmed that it is still used. In 2017 Gary Illyes wrote: “DYK that after 18 years we’re still using PageRank (and 100s of other signals) in ranking?” [6] A more specific description came out of the US antitrust case against Google. Notes of a February 2025 call with Google engineer HJ Kim, published by the Justice Department as a trial exhibit, read: “PageRank. This is a single signal relating to distance from a known good source, and it is used as an input to the Quality score.” The same notes list anchors, “a source page pointing to a target page (links)”, among the key components of topicality. [7] Read literally, that describes a measure of how far a page sits, through links, from sources Google considers good. The notes do not say how internal links are weighed against external ones. PageRank is also an internal Google measure. The authority scores sold by SEO tools are something else, as the Domain Authority myth explains.

Links are unlikely to count equally either. A Google patent filed in 2004 and granted in 2010 describes a “reasonable surfer model” in which “the surfer will follow some of the links with higher probability than others”. A link’s weight could depend on features such as the font size of its anchor text, its position on the page and the words in it, and the patent names “‘Terms of Service’ links, banner advertisements” as links unlikely to be followed. [8] A patent shows what Google engineers designed, not what runs in Search today, and Google Patents lists this one as expired. It is still the clearest public statement of an idea most SEOs assume: a link in the main content and a link in a footer list of 200 are not equivalent.

Trying to steer this with nofollow stopped working long ago. In 2009 Matt Cutts, then head of Google’s webspam team, explained that Google had changed how PageRank flows. A nofollowed link still counts when a page’s PageRank is divided among its links, so it no longer leaves more for the others. His advice on internal links: “In general, I would let PageRank flow freely within your site.” [9]

Click depth is set by templates

Google has said for years that distance from the home page matters more than URL structure. “From our point of view we don’t count slashes in the URLs,” Mueller said in 2018. “What does matter for us a little bit is how easy it is to actually find the content.” A store page one click from the home page “tells us that these stores are probably pretty relevant”. [10] In his 2021 answer, he said Google often starts with the home page, and “things that are linked from the home page we would see as being more important than things which are just like i don’t know five or six steps distance from the home page.” [3]

Neither statement sets a threshold, and “a little bit” is not a claim about a major ranking factor. With the PageRank arithmetic, though, they point the same way. Pages deep in the structure are found later, and the links that reach them carry less.

On large sites, depth is not chosen page by page. It falls out of templates. Figure 2 is a worked example: the same 10,000 products, 20 per listing page, under four common structures.

Figure 2. Worked example, not measured data. Shortest click path from the home page to each of 10,000 products listed 20 per page. The home page links to one top category; pagination in B and C links two pages either side plus the first and last page. Calculated by the author.

With only “next” and “previous” links, the deepest product sits 501 clicks from the home page, and half the products sit more than 251 clicks deep. Numbered pagination that links two pages either side, plus the first and last page, cuts the maximum to 127. Splitting the catalogue into 20 subcategories brings it to 10. Two levels of category pages, each holding 20 products, put every product four clicks from the home page. The products did not change. The architecture did.

Pagination itself is not the problem. Google retired rel="prev" and rel="next" as an indexing signal in 2019. “We don’t use link-rel-next/prev at all,” Mueller wrote at the time. [11] Paginated pages are ordinary URLs now. “Why shouldn’t a URL be a URL?” Mueller asked in 2025. [12] That leaves the links between them to connect a deep page to the rest of the site. A long paginated series is a chain, and each page in it sits one step further from the home page than the last.

Google does not crawl only from the home page. It also finds URLs in sitemaps, in external links and in its memory of URLs it has seen before. Click depth is a proxy for how prominently a site links to a page, not a literal crawl path. As a proxy, it is easy to measure and hard to argue with.

What controlled tests show

Most evidence about internal links is correlational. The best causal evidence comes from SEO split tests, in which similar pages are divided into control and variant groups and only the variant pages change. SearchPilot, a company that runs such tests for clients, has published several on internal linking.

The largest effect came from a grocery retailer. Buttons linking second-level category pages to the third-level categories beneath them raised organic traffic across both levels by 25%, an estimated 9,200 extra sessions a month. SearchPilot put it down to “clearer crawl paths”. [13] On a site with about 8,000 regional pages, linking each page to its six nearest neighbours gave the pages that gained links 7% more organic traffic. “These pages were starved of link equity,” SearchPilot wrote. [14]

Other results were weaker. Raising the number of related-article links on a retail content hub from two to four lifted the pages carrying the links by 16% at 95% confidence, but showed “no detectable impact on the recipient pages”. [15] Expanding a home page footer from 30 links to more than 100 gave the newly linked pages a 5% uplift that was not significant at 95% confidence; the desktop result alone was 10% and significant. [16] On a travel site, shortening a link block and hiding some links behind a “Show more” button gave a predicted 10.2% uplift, inconclusive at 95% confidence. [17] On another travel site, a block of links to the 30 most popular destinations made no noticeable difference in two tests. SearchPilot suggested the linked pages may already have been strong. [18]

Figure 3. Published SearchPilot split tests that changed internal links, by measured page group. Vendor-reported; one site per test; two tests do not state a confidence level, and SearchPilot chooses which tests to publish. Sources: [13]–[18].

The pattern is plausible but not proven. Links helped most where the target pages had few of them, and least where the targets were already prominent. The tests come from one vendor, cover one site each and do not always state confidence levels. They are also a selection, because SearchPilot decides which results to write up. Measurement itself is hard. SearchPilot’s own summary warns that moving links around can be “a zero-sum game where the overall site authority remains unchanged”, and says the effect on pages further down the chain is “hard (if not impossible) to measure”. [19]

Larger observational data shows the same shape with weaker evidence. Cyrus Shepard’s Zyppy study of 23 million internal links on 1,800 sites compared link counts with Google Search Console clicks. URLs with 0–4 internal links averaged 2 clicks; URLs with 40–44 links received four times as many. After about 45–50 links, the relationship reversed. URLs with more varied anchor text also drew more traffic. [20] The study calls itself “mostly a correlation study”, and a later note on the page says new data suggests that some sites with high anchor-text diversity “may suffer”. [20] Pages with many internal links are often the pages a site already considers important, so the arrow can point either way.

Where large sites lose link value

At scale, the common failures are structural, and each one is repeated on every page that uses the template.

Sitewide links spread importance thin. Mega menus and footers link every page to the same set of URLs. That helps the top categories and dilutes everything else. Google offers no number: “There’s no magical ideal number of links a given page should contain. However, if you think it’s too much, then it probably is.” [1] In the Zyppy data, sitewide links “often point to URLs with lower traffic”, although large high-traffic sites “tended to perform very well with navigational links”. [20]

Faceted navigation multiplies URLs. Google’s documentation says the common parameter-based implementation “can generate infinite URL spaces”, and that when crawling is spent on useless URLs, “the crawlers have less time to spend on new, useful URLs.” [21] Each filter link also takes a share of the value of the page it sits on. How large the crawl cost gets is covered in the crawl budget study.

Orphan pages are found but not backed. A product that appears only in a sitemap can be crawled, but nothing on the site says it matters, and Google’s advice on importance points to links, not sitemaps. [3] A URL Google knows about but does not prioritise can stay in the “Discovered – currently not indexed” state described in the crawlability vs. indexability study.

Migrations reset the link graph. When URLs change, every internal link that still names an old address points Google at a redirect. Those links are one of the leftover signals that keep old URLs alive, as the migration study describes. On a large site they sit in templates, feeds and old content, and a crawl finds them faster than a review does.

Auditing architecture at scale

On the large sites I have audited, up to around two million URLs, internal linking problems rarely show up in a page-by-page review. They show up in distributions. A crawl from the home page gives every URL a click depth and a count of internal links pointing to it. Grouped by template, those numbers show which page types sit deep, which are reached only through pagination, and which absorb links from every page through navigation.

The most revealing comparison is between three lists: the URLs a crawl finds by following links, the URLs in the sitemaps, and the URLs Googlebot requests in the server logs. URLs in the sitemaps but missing from the link crawl are orphans. URLs in the logs but in neither list are usually parameters, old addresses or filter combinations reached through links nobody meant to expose. Each gap points to a different template problem.

The changes that move results are therefore template changes: an extra level of category pages, links from parent to child categories, related-item modules on pages that have none, pagination that reaches deep pages in fewer steps, and fewer sitewide links to pages that do not need them. They change thousands of pages at once. That is also why they can be tested in split tests instead of argued about.

Conclusion

On a large site, internal linking is architecture. Links let Google discover URLs, and they tell Google which pages the site considers important. A sitemap handles the first job. Only links handle the second. Google has said so directly, and the court-filed description of PageRank as distance from a known good source is consistent with it.

The evidence on rankings is real but bounded. Published split tests show gains of 7% to 25% where under-linked pages received links, and little or nothing where the targets were already strong. Correlation studies show the same shape with less certainty. Internal links redistribute importance the site already has. The architecture decides where it goes: how many clicks separate a product from the home page, how many links share each page’s value, and whether the templates point at the pages the business needs to rank or at filters, footers and old URLs.
