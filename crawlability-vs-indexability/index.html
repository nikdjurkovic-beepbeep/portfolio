Crawlability vs. Indexability: Why Getting a Page Crawled Doesn’t Mean Getting It Indexed

Crawling is a fetch. Indexing is a decision. Google says plainly that not every page it processes will be indexed, and the pages it crawls but leaves out are usually duplicates or pages it judged not worth storing, not technical failures.

Key takeaways

  • Crawling and indexing are separate stages with separate controls. robots.txt governs crawling. noindex, canonicalisation and Google’s own judgement of the page govern indexing. Google states plainly that indexing isn’t guaranteed.
  • The controls cannot stand in for each other. A URL blocked in robots.txt can still be indexed from links, and a noindex tag on a blocked page is never seen.
  • Most crawled-but-not-indexed URLs are not broken. Google keeps its index deliberately smaller than the web, folds duplicates into one canonical and selects the rest on quality. In November 2026 it rewrote its help text to say the status “doesn’t necessarily indicate an issue”.
  • Scale changes the reading. A few such URLs are normal. Most of a site’s important pages in that state is, in Google’s own staff’s words, a sign its quality systems are unconvinced about the site.
  • Indexing is not permanent. In vendor data from 18 sites, none of the monitored pages were still indexed once Google had gone about five months without recrawling them.

Two gates, not one

“Crawlability” and “indexability” are often used as if they described one property. They describe two different gates.

Google describes Search in three stages: crawling, indexing and serving. “Not all pages make it through each stage,” its guide says. [1] Crawling is a fetch. Googlebot downloads a URL it has found through links, sitemaps or other means, and it “doesn’t crawl all the pages it discovered”. [1] Indexing is what happens to the fetched content. Google analyses the page, decides whether it duplicates another one, collects signals about it and may store it. The guide is explicit: “Indexing isn’t guaranteed; not every page that Google processes will be indexed.” It lists low-quality content, robots meta rules and site design among the common reasons. [1]

A crawlable page is one Googlebot can reach and fetch. An indexable page is one that, once fetched, carries nothing that rules it out. Neither guarantees the next step. A page can pass both tests and still stay out, because the final step is a choice Google makes, not a property of the page.

The distinction matters because the two gates fail in different places. A crawl problem lives in the server, robots.txt, internal links and crawl scheduling. An indexing problem lives in the page and in what Google thinks of it. Treating one as the other wastes most of the work that follows.

robots.txt and noindex do different jobs

The clearest sign that the gates are separate is that their controls cannot replace each other.

robots.txt controls crawling. Google’s documentation says it “is not a mechanism for keeping a web page out of Google”. If other pages link to a blocked URL, “Google could still index the URL without visiting the page”, and the result appears without a description. [3] The Page indexing report in Search Console has a status for exactly this case, “Indexed, though blocked by robots.txt”: “Google always respects robots.txt, but this doesn’t necessarily prevent indexing if someone else links to your page.” [2]

noindex controls indexing, and it works only if the page is crawled. “For the noindex rule to be effective, the page or resource must not be blocked by a robots.txt file,” Google says. Otherwise “the crawler will never see the noindex rule, and the page can still appear in search results”. A noindex rule placed inside robots.txt is not supported. [4]

The combination that looks safest is the one that fails: a page blocked in robots.txt and tagged noindex. The block hides the tag. That is why leftover development blocks do so much damage, as the migration study describes. It is also why adding robots.txt rules can stall an index cleanup. A page that is already indexed has to be crawled again before Google can see its noindex, and Google notes that a revisit can take months. [4]

Figure 2. What Google can do with a URL under each combination of robots.txt and noindex. The highlighted cell is the common mistake: the block stops Google from reading the noindex. Sources: [3][4][2].

Not every URL that is indexed though blocked is a problem. In June 2026, a WooCommerce site owner on Reddit found more than 51,000 URLs in that status, mostly add-to-cart links. John Mueller of Google answered: “Blocking them with robots.txt is fine.” Even if they get “indexed”, he added, “it’s unlikely that they’ll be shown in search”. [5] A blocked URL nobody searches for is a report entry, not a ranking problem. It matters when the URL had to stay out for a real reason, such as private or duplicate content, or when it competes with the page that should rank.

Rendering adds a third layer. When Google finds a noindex tag in the original page code, it “may skip rendering and JavaScript execution”, so a script written to remove the tag “may not work as expected”. [6] Other ways the raw and rendered HTML can disagree are covered in what Google indexes on JavaScript sites.

The index is selective by design

Technical controls explain part of the gap between crawled and indexed. Much of the rest comes from selection.

Google’s index is large, but it is deliberately smaller than the web Google sees. Testifying in the US antitrust case against Google in October 2023, Google’s Pandu Nayak described “a culling process starting even with crawling from the trillions of documents to the hundreds of billions”. [7] Asked how Google builds its index, he said: “It’s not even clear you want an index of the whole web, because the web has a lot of spam in it.” The goal is “an index of sort of the useful parts of the web”, because “if you fill your index with spam, then you’re not really going to help your users.” [7]

He also confirmed that the number of documents in the index has fallen at times, because documents grew larger: “for the same size of storage, you can index fewer documents”. [7] Every stored page has a cost, and Google manages that cost.

Gary Illyes has described the last step of indexing in the same terms. “The final step in indexing is deciding whether to include the page in Google’s index,” he said in a 2024 Google video. “This process, called index selection, largely depends on the page’s quality and the previously collected signals.” [8] In 2021, asked on Reddit whether requesting indexing in Search Console works, he wrote that it is “not a guarantee. the content still has to pass quality checks to get selected for indexing.” [9]

Selection works against a threshold, and pages near it move. Illyes said later that year that a page which drops out and returns after a manual submission is likely “very close to, but still above the quality threshold below which Google doesn’t index pages”. Mueller added: “If you’re teetering on the edge of indexing, there’s always fluctuation.” [10] A good page is not automatically safe either. “A page can be of high quality and still not be indexed - it’s not guaranteed,” Mueller wrote in 2023, because “there’s just a lot of good (& also questionable) stuff on the web”. [11]

That is the reason the gap exists at all. Crawling a page is how Google finds out what it is. Storing it is a separate decision, made after the fetch.

Duplicates are crawled, then folded

Duplication is the clearest legitimate reason a crawled page stays out. During indexing, Google clusters pages that look the same or very similar and picks one as canonical: the page that “is objectively the most complete and useful for search users”. Redirects, rel="canonical" annotations, sitemaps and HTTPS all feed the choice, but a declared canonical is “a hint, not a rule”. The other URLs in the cluster are usually not shown as separate results, and “duplicates are crawled less frequently”. [12]

That is the intended outcome, and the Page indexing report says so. “Your goal is to get the canonical version of every important page indexed. Duplicate or alternate pages shouldn’t be indexed.” Among the things not to look for, it adds: “You should not expect all URLs on your site to be indexed, only the canonical pages”. [2] The same mechanism lies behind the duplicate content penalty myth. Duplicates are filtered, not punished.

Problems start when Google’s choice differs from the site’s. “Duplicate, Google chose different canonical than user” means “Google has indexed the page that we consider canonical rather than this one.” [2] That is an indexability problem in the strict sense: the page was crawled, nothing excluded it, and another URL won the cluster. A common cause is conflicting signals: internal links, sitemaps or hreflang pointing at one URL while the canonical tag names another.

“Crawled – currently not indexed” causes the most worry, and Google rewrote its description in November 2026. The old text said the page “may or may not be indexed in the future; no need to resubmit this URL for crawling.” The new text says: “Google doesn’t index all pages across the web, so this is a common message for many sites and doesn’t necessarily indicate an issue that needs attention.” For important pages, it points to the URL Inspection tool to rule out blocks, and to a bug report only for “a complete, site-wide problem with indexing”. [13][2]

“Discovered – currently not indexed” belongs to the crawl stage. Google knows the URL but has not fetched it, and the help text blames expected server load. [2] Mueller said in 2022 that the status “can be forever”, that “it’s completely normal for any website that we don’t have everything indexed”, and that waiting alone will not change it. Google crawls and indexes more “when we see that there’s actually value in focusing more on the website itself”. [14] How crawl demand drives that is covered in the crawl budget study.

The documentation and Google’s staff now say two things that sound different. The help text calls the status common and not necessarily a problem. In a July 2026 episode of Google’s Search Off the Record podcast, Martin Splitt asked whether “crawled – currently not indexed” is “often or only sometimes a sign of a quality issue”. Mueller answered “Sometimes”, and said that when Google’s systems are seriously worried about a site’s quality, “we’ll probably crawl a lot less, we’ll index a lot less.” [15] About a week after the change was reported, a site owner asked Mueller about a site with a large share of pages in the crawled and discovered states. His reply: “Usually this is a sign that Google’s quality systems are unconvinced about your site (or convinced in the way that you don’t want).” [16]

The two positions fit once scale is added. A few hundred crawled-but-not-indexed URLs on a large site are normal selection at the margin. Most of a site’s important pages in that state is a judgement about the site. “If a tiny number of your sites pages are indexed,” Mueller said, “that suggests either a severe technical issue or that Google just isn’t convinced about your site overall.” He also dismissed the usual shortcut: “Just making the site smaller doesn’t significantly raise the overall quality of the site.” [16]

Resubmitting does not settle it. “Requesting a crawl does not guarantee that inclusion in search results will happen instantly or even at all,” Google’s recrawl guide says, and repeated requests for the same URL “won’t get it crawled any faster”. [17] A recrawl fetches the page again. It does not change the decision unless the page, or Google’s view of the site, has changed.

Indexed is not permanent

Index selection is not a one-time decision. Pages enter and leave the index as Google re-evaluates them. In late May 2025, several site owners reported pages dropping out of Google’s index. Mueller found no technical issue on Google’s side and said: “Our systems make adjustments in what’s crawled & indexed regularly. That’s normal and expected.” Later he added: “We don’t index all content, and what we index can change over time.” [18]

The most detailed public data on how recrawling and continued indexing relate comes from Indexing Insight, a company that sells index-monitoring software. In 2025 it compared the last crawl time reported by Google’s URL Inspection API with the index status of 1.4 million monitored pages on 18 websites. Pages crawled in the previous 30 days had a 97% chance of being indexed. Between 100 and 130 days since the last crawl, 85–94% were indexed. After 131 days the share fell sharply, and after 151 days none of the monitored pages were indexed. [19]

Vendor study of 18 sites and 1.4 million pages their owners chose to monitor, with technical and duplicate errors excluded. Source: [19].

The study has clear limits. It covers 18 sites that use the tool, and only the pages their owners chose to monitor. It filters out technical and duplication errors. The vendor sells the monitoring its finding encourages. And it is observational. A long gap since the last crawl probably does not cause deindexing on its own. More likely, both reflect the same thing: Google has lost interest in the URL. The study itself calls its 130-day threshold “more of an indicator than a hard rule”. [19]

Read that way, the finding fits Google’s own account. Crawl frequency follows demand, and demand follows Google’s view of the page. A URL Google stops revisiting is usually one it has stopped valuing, and the index catches up.

Reading the gap on a large site

A sitewide indexing percentage says little. Asked whether a high share of non-indexed pages is a bad sign, Mueller said: “There is no percentage.” He called it “normal that not all URLs are indexed”, said “it really depends on the site, the setup, the value”, and gave the example of an endless calendar, where many dates would never be indexed. [20]

On the large sites I have audited, up to around two million URLs, the useful view is by URL group: template, directory and market. Faceted and parameter URLs, internal search results and deep archive pages are expected to sit in the excluded statuses, and many of them should. Product, category and article templates are expected to be indexed. The same “crawled – currently not indexed” count means different things in those two groups. In the first, it is usually the system working as designed. In the second, it measures how many of the pages the business needs Google has fetched and declined.

The split also separates crawl work from indexing work. Crawl problems show up as URLs Google has not fetched: discovered-not-indexed, server errors, robots.txt blocks and long gaps since the last crawl. Indexing problems show up as URLs Google has fetched and turned down: crawled-not-indexed, soft 404s and lost canonical clusters. The first group responds to infrastructure, rules and links. The second responds only when the pages, or the site, become worth indexing.

The stakes have grown. To appear as a supporting link in AI Overviews or AI Mode, Google says, “a page must be indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet”, and “there are no additional technical requirements.” [21] The index is the gate for those features as well, which is why the SEO vs GEO study treats AI visibility as an extension of SEO rather than a separate discipline.

Conclusion

A crawled page has passed one gate. Crawlability means Google can fetch the URL. Indexability means nothing on the page rules it out. Neither means Google will keep it. The last step is index selection, which Google describes openly as a judgement on quality, duplication and collected signals, applied to an index it keeps deliberately smaller than the web.

That changes what the common Search Console statuses mean. Blocks, server errors and conflicting controls are real technical faults, and they can be found and fixed. Most crawled-but-not-indexed URLs are something else: duplicates folded into a canonical, or pages Google judged not worth storing. A handful of them is normal. Most of a site’s important templates in that state is a verdict on the site, and no amount of resubmission or crawl tuning reverses it.
