Evidence review · Generative Engine Optimization

What GEO research actually shows about AI citations.

The clearest recent evidence points to a measured conclusion: traditional search visibility remains an important baseline, while the added effect of page-level GEO tactics is smaller and less certain than many claims suggest.

By Nikola DjurkovicNovember 17, 20269 minute read
In brief

AI search visibility is a multi-stage process. Ranking well in Google is strongly associated with citation odds in one large observational study, but citations from beyond the top 30 still make up most events because the long tail is huge. The evidence does not support rebuilding a well-optimized site around generic GEO hacks.

The question behind GEO

Generative Engine Optimization is often presented as a new set of website changes: restructure every page into short answers, add more schema, publish an llms.txt file, or rewrite headings for AI systems. The important question is whether those changes improve discovery and citation beyond what strong SEO already achieves.

That distinction matters. A page can be accessible to a search engine, retrieved as a source, cited in an answer, influence the answer, and eventually bring a visitor to a site. Each is a different outcome. A higher citation count does not automatically mean more qualified traffic or revenue.

My reading of the evidence: GEO currently has a stronger case as a measurement and research discipline than as a broadly proven, separate website optimization method. Good SEO remains the floor; specific GEO interventions need to earn their place through controlled results.

What the 2026 study measured

Anthony Lee's 2026 working paper, The SEO Floor: Measuring Google Rank Distribution of AI-Cited Pages, analyzed 100,411 citation events from ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude, and Google AI Mode across 2,000 queries. The authors compared cited URLs with Google search results for those same queries and modeled citation odds by rank tier and seven content features.[1]

This is unusually useful because it separates two questions that are often blended together: where cited pages rank in Google, and whether selected page features add predictive information after rank is considered.

The paper is observational. It can identify associations in its sample, but it cannot show that a particular SEO or GEO change caused a page to be cited. The authors also say that schema may be a proxy for other aspects of site quality; the mechanism remains unresolved.[1]

The ranking and citation pattern

The study found that pages ranking in Google's top three had 7.82 times the citation odds of pages in positions 11–30. Pages in positions 31–100 had lower odds than that reference group (odds ratio 0.23). At the same time, 75.4% of citation events came from URLs outside Google's top 30.[1]

Those findings are compatible. The top-three result describes the odds for an individual page in a particular rank tier. The 75.4% figure counts all events in the much larger pool beyond rank 30. A very large long tail can contribute many citations even when any one page in it has lower odds.

Most of the study's outside-the-top-30 citations came from pages ranked beyond 100, rather than a dense band just below the first page. The authors also report that many of those deep-tier citations were one-off appearances. That makes a single AI answer a weak basis for declaring a stable visibility win.[1]

What page-level features add

The study's seven-feature GEO composite added only a small amount of predictive power beyond Google rank. Schema markup was the strongest individual association (odds ratio 1.31); statistics density and list structure showed smaller positive associations, while heading density showed a small negative association after the authors' corrections.[1]

An odds ratio is not a percentage-point lift in citation probability, and an association is not proof that adding markup causes citations. Pages with schema may also be better maintained, clearer, or more authoritative for reasons the model cannot fully isolate.

The foundational 2024 GEO paper reported gains of up to 40% in its experimental visibility metric. That is evidence that content presentation can affect results in the paper's benchmark setting; it does not establish a durable uplift in organic discovery, citations across platforms, traffic, or conversions.[2]

Why visibility needs stages

Olivier Martinez's 2026 critical survey reviewed 45 GEO studies and argues that visibility is not one result. It covers a partly observable sequence: a system decides whether to search, accesses and retrieves a page, selects or cites it, uses some of its information, and may or may not lead a person to act.[3]

The survey's conclusion is deliberately narrow: the reviewed evidence shows that document changes can affect how an already-retrieved source is cited or used in an answer, but it does not establish a technique with stable, longitudinal, cross-platform causal effects on organic discoverability or downstream behavior.[3]

Google's current guidance makes a similar distinction for its own AI features: ordinary search eligibility and SEO best practices continue to apply, and Google says there are no additional technical requirements or special optimizations for appearing in AI Overviews or AI Mode.[4]

Confidence ratings are a qualitative synthesis of the cited research, not a promise of performance. Google specifically says no special schema is required for its AI features; use structured data for supported search features and clear content description, not as an AI citation switch.[4]

Measure before changing a well-performing site

For a website that already has a strong technical and editorial foundation, the next step should be diagnosis rather than a wholesale rewrite. Build a repeatable sample of relevant questions, run it across the platforms that matter, record dates and model changes, and track which URLs appear and how consistently.

  • Separate brand mentions, linked citations, factual use, referral visits, and conversions.
  • Repeat prompts and vary their wording; one response can be a one-off.
  • Compare cited URLs with organic rankings and the site's existing search performance.
  • Test one change at a time against a control where possible, with a rollback and regression check.

If a recommendation does not name the stage it targets, the evidence for it, and the outcome it should improve, it is not yet a testable optimization plan.

Limits and conclusion

The evidence base is moving quickly. Studies use different engines, prompts, sampling methods, visibility metrics, and access conditions. Some results are observational; controlled benchmarks do not automatically transfer to live search systems. A reported citation can also vary from run to run.

That leaves a practical conclusion, not a new checklist of hacks: keep doing the SEO work that makes pages discoverable and valuable, then test specific changes against clear AI visibility measures. GEO is useful as a way to investigate where generative systems find and use sources. The research does not yet justify treating it as a replacement for SEO or as a reason to dismantle a well-engineered site.
