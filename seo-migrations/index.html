The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Website Migration: Where SEO Migrations Go Wrong

Google says permanent redirects lose no PageRank and that a clean site move can settle in weeks. Recovery data from more than a thousand domain migrations puts the median closer to ten months. The gap comes from how migrations are run, not from the redirects themselves.

Key takeaways

  • Google processes a migration one URL at a time. A move is complete only when Googlebot has visited every old and new URL, and permanent redirects themselves lose no PageRank.
  • The largest public dataset, 1,052 domain migrations collected by a migration agency, puts the median time for organic traffic to return to its pre-move level at 304 days. After three years, 13.9% still had not recovered.
  • The documented failures come from how migrations are run, not from the redirect mechanism: bad redirect maps, launches that change everything at once, leftover development blocks and old-URL signals, and redirects removed too early.
  • One-to-one moves are the easiest case for Google. Its own staff describe merges, splits and partial moves as slower and messier.

A migration is not one event

“Migration” covers several different projects. Google’s documentation separates moves that change URLs, such as a new domain, a switch to HTTPS or a new path structure, from moves that do not, such as a new hosting provider or a CDN. [1][2] The hard cases are large moves that change URLs, usually with a new platform and new templates attached.

From the outside, a migration looks like a launch date. From Google’s side, it is a long series of small events. “The move takes place on a per-URL basis,” the site move guide says, and to consider a move complete, “Googlebot will have to visit every URL on your old and new site at least once.” [1] Each old URL has to be recrawled, its redirect followed, and the new URL crawled and accepted as canonical. The site does not move. Its URLs do, one crawl at a time.

The redirect is the least risky part of that chain. “301 and other permanent redirects don’t cause a loss in PageRank,” Google says. [1] A permanent redirect is a signal that the target should be canonical, and a temporary one is not. [3] Google’s crawler documentation calls a 301 a “strong” signal and a 302 a “weak” one, and treats a 308 like a 301 and a 307 like a 302. [4] The cost is time. The guide warns: “Expect temporary fluctuation in site ranking during the move.” It says the speed depends largely on the number of URLs and the speed of the server. For medium-sized websites, “it can take a few weeks or more”, and for larger sites, “even longer”. [1]

Figure 1. How Google Search processes a migration, one URL at a time, and the failure points documented at each step. Sources: [1][3][4][5][6].

In October 2026, Gary Illyes showed internal timing ranges at Search Central Live in Barcelona. A site move took one to three months in the typical case and six months to more than a year in the slowest. A refresh crawl of a URL Google already knows took about 30 days in the typical case, and anything from weeks to never in the slowest. [6] Illyes later called the slides “an exercise to see if the audience can relate to the numbers we pulled internally”, and no method or sample was published.

John Mueller was more optimistic about the simplest case in 2019. After a domain move that follows every step in Google’s help documentation, “sometimes within a day or two things will just switch over.” In the same answer he added that “any bigger change of URLs within a website are always kind of a scary situation for me still.” [7]

What the recovery data shows

Traffic tells a slower story. The largest public dataset comes from Dan Taylor of SALT.agency, a technical SEO agency that sells migration services. In June 2026 he published recovery times for 1,052 domain migrations, some from SALT’s own work and the rest crowdsourced from other SEOs. Each migration is dated from the first month in which the old domain’s organic traffic fell below 40% of its six-month average. It counts as recovered in the first month the new domain reaches or beats that average. The data is monthly, so each figure carries about 30 days of error either way. [8]

The median recovery time was 304 days, and the mean was 489. Only 22.8% of migrations had recovered by day 90, and 59.5% by the end of the first year. After three years, 13.9% still had not. [8] The study’s headline says 27% recover in 90 days, but its own table puts 27.8% at day 120. An earlier study by the same author, of 892 domains and based mostly on Ahrefs traffic estimates, found an average of 523 days before the new domain matched the old one’s estimated traffic, and 17% that had not matched it after 1,000 days. [9]

Figure 2. Share of 1,052 domain migrations whose organic traffic had returned to its pre-move baseline, by time since the move. The shaded band is the typical time Google gave for processing a site move, which measures indexing, not traffic. Agency study, partly crowdsourced; monthly data, about ±30 days. Sources: [8][6].

Both datasets have real limits. They cover domain changes only, not replatforms or URL changes on the same domain. They rely on traffic estimates or on data the study does not describe; the 2026 write-up does not name its traffic source. Crowdsourced cases may lean towards memorable migrations, good or bad. A strict return to a pre-move baseline also counts seasonality, market decline and algorithm updates as a failure to recover. The 2026 study says it does not run a regression against migration quality factors, so it cannot say which mistakes caused the slow cases. [8] And the author sells the service that long recoveries make a case for.

Even so, the two datasets point the same way, and both sit closer to Google’s slowest range than its typical one. The gap is the useful part. Processing measures whether Google has moved the URLs. Traffic measures whether the moved URLs perform as well as the old ones did. The difference is where the rest of the project shows up.

Where migrations go wrong

The public record of failed migrations is thin, because companies rarely publish their own. What exists comes from visibility tools that watch domains from outside, from Google staff answering site owners’ questions, and from Google’s troubleshooting notes. Together they point to six failure points. The redirect mechanism is not one of them.

1. The redirect map

Every old URL needs a decision: a new equivalent, a consolidated page that replaces several old ones, or an honest 404 or 410. Google asks for exactly that, and lists the ways it goes wrong. Redirects should point straight to the final destination. If a chain cannot be avoided, it should be “ideally no more than 3 and fewer than 5” hops, even though Googlebot follows up to 10. Many old URLs should not be sent to “one irrelevant single URL destination, such as the home page of the new site”, which “might be treated as a soft 404 error”. [1][4]

Lush shows the cost of getting this wrong. After the company moved its UK site from lush.co.uk to lush.com, SISTRIX recorded its Google UK visibility falling from 8.808 points in December 2015 to 1.646 in March 2016, a loss of more than 75%. Checking the old URLs, SISTRIX found that “most of these redirects end in 404 pages”. [10]

At scale, maps are rarely written by hand. On migrations of the size I have led, up to around 500,000 URLs, the map is built from patterns, and patterns break at the edges: legacy paths, parameter variants, and URLs that survive only in old backlinks and old internal links. The redirect type matters too. A 302 used for a permanent move gives Google only a weak signal, so the old URL can stay in the index. JavaScript redirects are a last resort, because “Google might never see it if rendering of the content failed”. [3][4] A move onto a JavaScript framework adds the risks described in where JavaScript sites lose pages.

2. Too many changes at once

Google’s guide is short on this point: “Plan your changes to your site one after the other, not everything at the same time.” [1] Its Change of Address documentation adds that “keeping the same site architecture in the new location helps to pass the signals more directly to the new site.” [5]

Clarks did the opposite in January 2020. It folded its Spanish, French, German and Italian country domains into folders on clarks.eu and moved to a new e-commerce platform at the same time. SISTRIX measured visibility falling by almost 97% in Spain, 92% in France and 61% in Germany. Its audit found redirects that sent users and Googlebot through the English version, chains of up to four or five hops, and hreflang problems that left 61% of keywords ranking with a URL meant for another market. SISTRIX’s summary was blunt: “Too many changes all at the same time.” [11]

Figure 3. Visibility lost after three migrations documented by SISTRIX, from the last level before each move to a low point after it. Only the Guardian’s move is recorded as recovered. Sources: [10][11].

Bundling costs twice. Each change asks Google to re-evaluate something: a new template changes internal links, titles, canonicals and structured data on thousands of pages at once, and markup a template drops disappears everywhere without a ranking drop to announce it, as the schema markup study describes. And when the domain, platform, templates and content all change on the same day, a drop cannot be traced to a cause, so the fixes become guesses.

A migration also does not reset a site. In 2022, an SEO told Mueller about a site that had lost about 90% of its clicks and impressions, moved to a new domain, ranked well there at first, and dropped again after three days. Mueller’s answer: “that sounds a lot like a quality issue”. [12] A move transfers signals, including the ones the owner hoped to leave behind.

3. Development settings that ship

New sites are built behind blocks. Google notes that “some site owners block all crawling while in development”, and its troubleshooting list for site moves includes a line that should not need writing: “Don’t forget to remove any noindex or robots.txt blocks that were only needed for the migration.” [1] Its hosting guide makes the same point, and adds that the Search Console verification file has to come across to the new copy of the site. [2]

Quieter leftovers point at the old site. Google asks for a self-referencing canonical on every new URL, a sitemap of new URLs, updated hreflang and internal links changed to the new addresses. [1] Each one that still names an old URL is a vote for an address that now redirects. The redirect is a strong signal, but canonical selection weighs several, and a site that keeps linking to its old URLs sends mixed ones. Sophie Brannon of the agency StudioHawk lists leftover noindex tags and canonicals that point to old URLs among the causes of what she calls a migration hangover. From her agency’s work, she puts a normal temporary dip at 10–30% of traffic and a damaging one at 50% or more. [13] Those ranges are practitioner judgement, not published data.

4. Crawl capacity on large sites

A migration creates more crawling, not less. Google says that “after a migration, Google will temporarily crawl your new site more heavily than usual”, and that the new site needs the capacity to handle it. [1] Server errors work against that. 5xx responses and 429s “prompt Google’s crawlers to temporarily slow down with crawling”, and URLs that keep returning server errors are eventually removed from the index. [4] Even a hosting change with no URL changes can bring “a temporary drop in Googlebot’s crawl rate immediately after the launch”. [2]

On a large site, crawl demand sets the pace of the long tail. Popular URLs are recrawled quickly and move first. URLs that Google rarely visits move when Google next visits them, and Illyes’ slowest range for refreshing a known URL runs from weeks to never. [6] How server capacity and demand set that pace is covered in the crawl budget study. The old URLs also stay on Google’s schedule after the move. Mueller said in 2021 that Google will keep retrying old redirected URLs “for years, on and off”, at a much slower rate, and that this “doesn’t cause issues with the rest of the site’s SEO”. [14]

5. Merges, splits and partial moves

Google handles one-to-one moves best. Mueller said in 2018: “Moving sites is generally a lot easier because we can pass all of the signals one to one.” By contrast, “Splitting and merging sites is always really tricky for us.” [15] He repeated the point in 2024: “Splitting & merging tend to take much longer than a normal site migration.” [16] The Change of Address documentation warns that “Moving sites A, B, and C all to new location D can cause some confusion and traffic loss.” Mueller has said the same of merging individual pages: “Merging is always a bit more challenging than a simple site-move”. [5][17]

Partial moves are the messiest version. In December 2025, a site owner asked about a Change of Address where only the home page was moving to the new domain. Mueller replied: “Practically speaking, it’s not going to be seen as a full site move,” and “it will be a messy situation until you’ve really moved it all over.” [18] That is different from a planned move in stages. Google recommends moving small and medium sites all at once, but says large sites can move “one section at a time”. [1] The line runs between a large site moved in planned sections and a domain left with half its pages on each side.

6. Cleaning up too early

The last failure comes months after launch. The Change of Address tool runs for 180 days. Google asks site owners to keep redirects for at least that long, “longer if you still see any traffic to them from Google Search”, and says that after the 180 days it “does not recognize any relationship between the old and new sites”. It also recommends paying for the old domain for at least a year. [5] The main guide is more cautious: “Keep the redirects for as long as possible, generally at least 1 year.” From users’ perspective, it suggests keeping them indefinitely. [1] When a site owner described a move with redirects kept for three months, Mueller answered: “3 months is too short for a site-move.” [19]

The evidence on removing redirects later is thin. In 2023, Ahrefs removed redirects to four of its own articles to test Gary Illyes’ 2021 statement that redirect signals stay consolidated after a year. Referring domains counted for the articles fell. Traffic rose on one, fell on one and stayed flat on two, and the fall began before the redirects were removed. Patrick Stox concluded that the claim “seems to mostly hold true”, but was “not willing to conclusively say that permanent redirects pass value even after one year”. [20] Four pages on one site cannot settle the question. They do show the asymmetry. Keeping a redirect costs almost nothing. Removing one saves almost nothing and risks the links that still point at the old URL.

Reading a migration too early

Many migrations are judged before Google has finished processing them. Google expects the old URLs to linger: “it’s very likely that Google will continue to occasionally show the old URLs in the results”. [3] In October 2026, a site owner reported that about four months after a domain change, Google had restored the old domain’s visibility and stopped indexing the new one. Mueller’s answer was that “Sometimes things just take a particularly long time to be fully processed.” He also suggested setting up the Change of Address “for both www and non-www”. [21]

The unit of measurement matters as much as the date. Because the move happens per URL, a sitewide traffic line hides where the losses sit. A stable total can mask one template that lost most of its traffic while seasonal pages grew. Large migrations are easier to read by URL group, such as template, directory and market, alongside the share of each group’s old URLs that Google has recrawled and replaced. Most of the failures above show up in that view weeks before they show up in revenue.

Conclusion

Large migrations rarely go wrong because Google mishandles redirects. Permanent redirects pass PageRank, and Google processes a clean one-to-one move reliably, if not quickly. They go wrong in how the move is run: in maps that send old URLs to errors or the home page, in launches that change the domain, platform and templates on the same day, in leftover blocks and old-URL signals, in servers that slow Google down when it most needs to crawl, and in redirects removed too soon.

The recovery data is commercial and imperfect, but it points one way. Traffic took a median of about ten months to return to its pre-move level in the largest public sample, and a minority never got there. Simple moves sit at the fast end. The Guardian’s 2013 move from guardian.co.uk to theguardian.com cut its visibility roughly in half, and it was back at its previous level within three months. [10] The documented slow cases involve the failures listed here, and almost all of them can be found before launch.
