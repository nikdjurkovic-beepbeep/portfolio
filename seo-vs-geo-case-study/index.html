GEO – Snake Oil, Just Good SEO, or Something Else?

How generative search changes visibility—and why most of the optimization work remains SEO.

Key takeaways

  • GEO has not replaced SEO. Generative search adds a new retrieval and citation layer, along with new ways to measure visibility. The foundations of search optimization remain the same.
  • Most techniques sold as GEO are familiar SEO: technical SEO, relevant content, clear site structure, consistent entity information, original research and off-page authority.
  • Some of this work needs to account for how AI systems find information, select passages and build answers. That is SEO adapting to a new search environment, not a separate discipline with its own foundations.
  • AI visibility is easy to market, so familiar SEO work is being sold under a new name. The label does not make old techniques new, and it does not prove that every GEO tactic works.
  • The important distinction is between established SEO, changes needed for generative retrieval and tactics that lack evidence. Citations, referral traffic and business results also need separate measures.

GEO is SEO adapting to generative search

SEO has a habit of giving established work new names. GEO is the latest example. Crawlability, indexing, relevance, internal links, useful content, structured data, digital PR and reputation management were all part of SEO before the current AI search market. Most techniques now sold as GEO would once have been called good SEO.

There are real changes, though. Generative search can rewrite a question, run several searches, retrieve and reorder pages, select passages and build an answer with citations. The pages it cites do not always rank for the original query. Different platforms can return different sources, and the same platform can give different answers on separate runs.

That creates extra SEO work: checking which sources get retrieved, understanding query rewrites, making key information easy to find on a page, and tracking citations alongside search performance. None of this replaces SEO’s foundations. Information still needs to be accessible, relevant, clear and credible. What changes is how search systems find and present it.

The new label makes routine SEO sound like a new specialty. The genuinely additional work is narrower: testing query rewrites and platform-specific retrieval, checking which sources are selected, and tracking citations. A new interface and new reporting needs do not create a new discipline. Most of the work remains SEO.

The original GEO study measured how content is used—not whether websites can be discovered

Aggarwal and colleagues introduced GEO in a paper presented at KDD in 2024. They built GEO-BENCH, a set of 10,000 queries, and tested nine ways to change source content. Adding statistics, quotations and citations improved some visibility measures. The strongest methods reported gains of up to 41% on the paper’s position-adjusted word-count metric. [1]

That figure is often presented as proof that a website can increase its AI visibility by 40%. The study did not show that. GEO-BENCH gave the generative engine the top five Google results for each query. The researchers changed one source text and measured how the answer used it. They tested what happened after a page had already been selected. They did not test whether the edited page could be found, crawled, indexed or retrieved from the open web.

The result supports a narrower point: once a system has a source, the way its information is presented can affect how much of it appears in the answer. Clear writing, useful statistics and credible quotations can help. The study does not show that these changes make a page more likely to be retrieved from the open web, or that a site should expect a 40% increase in visibility.

Martinez’s 2026 survey reviewed 45 studies and reached a similar conclusion. Some changes affect how systems use or cite content they have already retrieved. But the research does not yet show a lasting effect across platforms on whether websites are discovered or what users do next. The survey is a preprint, and the studies it reviews use different methods and measures. [2]

The original GEO paper helped define the problem and offered ways to measure visibility in AI answers. It did not show that SEO had been replaced. Many of its successful changes—adding useful data, citing sources and presenting information clearly—are standard editorial and SEO practices tested in a new setting.

Generative search adds a retrieval layer to SEO

Many GEO checklists focus on page format: summaries, question headings, short paragraphs, FAQs and schema. Some can make a page clearer or help it answer a query. None is a new discipline. The first question is simpler: does the system retrieve the page at all?

A generative search system may rewrite a question, run several searches, collect candidate pages, reorder them, select passages and write an answer. Platforms handle these steps differently, and some answers also draw on information stored in the model. But if a page is never retrieved, its formatting cannot help it appear in the answer.

Figure 1. A simplified view of how a generative search system can find sources and build an answer. The details vary by platform.

Chen and Koudas analysed 158,099 answers and 468,037 citations across two commercial markets. When the researchers compared pages on the same site, pages with titles and paragraphs that matched the questions received more citations. FAQ blocks, schema, summary boxes and page speed did not explain citation differences between pages on those sites. Some features showed associations across different sites, but those comparisons can also reflect differences in brand strength, authority and content quality. This is a 2026 preprint, not proof of ranking factors that apply across platforms. [3]

Their retrieval replay helps explain the result. In the ChatGPT workflow they tested, the system’s own search results explained 32% of the differences in citation frequency between pages on the same site. Page features explained 2.8%. Retrieval mattered far more than page-level formatting in that test. The study is a preprint, and its findings should not be treated as universal rules. [3]

Grossman and colleagues compared Google Search, AI Overviews and Gemini across 11,500 queries.

Figure 2. Average source overlap was below 0.20 on the Jaccard measure across Google Search, AI Overviews and Gemini in the SIGIR 2026 study.

Rankings, citations and prompt-tracking scores measure different things. They describe different stages and outputs of search, so they are not interchangeable. SEO needs retrieval testing and platform-specific reporting added to it. It does not need a separate checklist that ignores the work already being done.

Google rankings influence AI citations, but do not determine them

AI citations do not simply copy Google’s top ten. Ahrefs studied 15,000 prompts across ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot and Perplexity. On average, only 12% of the URLs cited by ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot also appeared in Google’s top ten for the same prompt. Perplexity was the outlier, with close to one-third of its citations appearing in Google’s top ten. The comparison used the original prompt, not necessarily the rewritten searches run by each AI system. [9]

Both findings can be true. Google AI Overviews and standalone AI products do not necessarily find sources in the same way. An AI system may also turn one question into several searches. Comparing its citations with Google rankings for the original wording can miss where a cited page ranked for the rewritten searches the system actually ran.

Anthony Lee’s observational study analysed 100,411 citation events across ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude and Google AI Mode. Pages in Google’s top three had 7.82 times the odds of being cited compared with pages ranked 11–30. Pages ranked 31–100 had lower odds, at 0.23 relative to the same baseline. The study found an association, not proof that Google rankings cause AI citations. [16]

The study also found that 75.4% of citations came from pages outside Google’s top 30. That figure needs context. There are far more pages beyond the top 30 than within it, so they can account for most citations even when each page has a much lower chance of being cited. Lee describes this as a denominator effect. The aggregate figure does not mean that low-ranking pages are more likely to be cited. [16]

Figure 3. Odds ratios from The SEO Floor study. Google ranking comparisons use positions 11–30 as the baseline. The GEO-feature score is an observed association, not proof of cause and effect.

The evidence does not support either extreme. A high Google ranking does not guarantee an AI citation, but one large study found that pages ranking well were more likely to be cited. AI systems can also find pages that rank poorly for the original query. Google rankings still matter. Generative retrieval adds another part of the process to investigate.

Seven established SEO practices that also matter in AI search

These are not seven new GEO techniques. They are established parts of content, technical and off-page SEO. They also matter in generative search because AI systems select and combine passages instead of simply showing ranked pages. Some have direct research support; others have a clear technical or editorial purpose. None guarantees a citation.

1. Match the page to the question

A page is easier to understand when the answer sits near the heading that introduces the question, headings describe the subject, and key facts are written in complete passages. Abbreviations should be explained and terms used consistently. This is clear writing and sound site structure. AI retrieval gives it another reason to matter.

That does not mean every page should be broken into short answers. The format should fit the subject. A product comparison needs clear criteria and a conclusion. A technical guide should explain requirements before the steps. A company page should say what the organisation does, where it operates and what sets it apart.

There is research behind this. Chen and Koudas found that titles and paragraphs matching the questions were associated with more citations on the sites they studied. Aggarwal and colleagues found that statistics and quotations could improve how a model used source material it had already been given. Neither study shows that answer-first formatting alone gets pages retrieved from the open web. The advice is familiar: answer the question clearly and make the relevant information easy to find. [1][3]

2. Back up important claims

Good factual content shows where its information comes from. It names primary sources, authors, dates and methods. Numbers need a source and a clear explanation of what they measure. Results, estimates, opinions and forecasts should not be mixed together. Important limitations should be stated plainly.

This gives search systems information they can extract and gives readers a way to check it. It also makes the page more useful than another summary of facts repeated across the web. Proper sourcing and editorial standards are not GEO inventions. They are part of credible publishing.

GEO-BENCH offers limited experimental support for using statistics and quotations in source material supplied to a model. The wider point is simple: sourced claims and clear methods make content more credible. AI search gives those qualities another way to affect visibility, but they were good SEO and publishing practice long before GEO had a name. Decorative or invented statistics are not evidence. [1]

3. Publish research others can cite

Original data, tests, benchmarks, comparisons, expert analysis and first-hand experience give a page something competitors cannot get by rewriting the same sources. Another version of generic advice adds little when dozens of pages already say the same thing.

A benchmark with a disclosed sample, a product test with repeatable conditions or a technical analysis with reproducible findings gives other publishers something concrete to reference. It also gives search systems information that is not interchangeable with another generic explanation.

This is an SEO advantage, not a GEO invention. Original research can attract links and mentions, and give a site a reason to be cited. It cannot guarantee AI citations. Its value is that it adds information and can build the independent coverage that supports a brand’s authority.

4. Keep entity information consistent

Names and details for products, companies, people, locations and technical concepts should be consistent. Their relationships should be clear too: who owns a product, which specification is current, what a service includes and how similar products differ. This is established entity and information-architecture work, not a new type of optimization.

Structured data is useful when it accurately describes what is visible on the page and supports a recognised search feature. It should match the page and its canonical version. Markup that describes no useful information adds code, not authority.

The evidence for schema as an AI citation factor is mixed. Chen and Koudas found that structured data did not explain citation differences between pages on the same site. Lee reported an association between schema and citations, but that does not prove that adding schema causes citations or that the result applies across platforms. Google says its AI features do not need special schema or AI-specific markup. Structured data still has a role in supported search features and accurate page descriptions. Calling it GEO does not create an extra benefit. [3][4][16]

5. Build authority beyond your own website

A brand’s website is only one source of information about it. AI systems can also find reviews, specialist publications, forums, reference sites, industry directories and competitor pages.

Chen and Koudas found that brand familiarity affected which products appeared in the ChatGPT workflow they tested. That does not tell marketers how to change a model’s internal knowledge. It does show that a brand’s wider reputation can matter beyond the content on its own website. The finding is specific to their test and should not be treated as a rule for every platform. [3]

The work itself is familiar off-page SEO: earn relevant links, get accurate coverage in specialist publications, maintain credible profiles, publish useful YouTube videos and build genuine customer reviews.

A brand mention is not the same as a linked citation. A mention can show that people know the brand. A citation means a source appeared in a particular answer. Neither proves that the system endorses the brand, recommends it or sent a visitor to the site.

6. Keep changing information up to date

Pages with changing information need regular checks. This includes prices, availability, legal requirements, software versions, ownership, leadership, product specifications and market data. Dates should reflect real publication or update work. Outdated claims need correcting; obsolete pages may need to be merged or redirected.

Freshness is not a ranking boost for every page. It matters when a query needs current information. Changing the date on a timeless explainer does not make it better. Updating a compatibility table after a software release does. This is normal content maintenance, applied to search systems that need current facts.

7. Make sure search crawlers can access the site

Each platform has its own crawler rules, but the technical checks are familiar: status codes, internal links, rendered content, robots.txt, noindex directives, canonical tags and redirect chains. Server logs show whether crawlers visit. A URL being live on a site does not mean a search system can access it.

Google says its AI features use the same basic technical requirements as Search: a page must be indexed and eligible to appear with a snippet. Google does not require special AI files, extra schema or a separate technical process for AI Overviews and AI Mode. Microsoft’s guidance also points to crawlability, indexing and content quality for Copilot. [4][5][6]

OpenAI lists separate crawlers for search and model training. OAI-SearchBot helps ChatGPT find content for search, while GPTBot relates to possible model training. Publishers can allow one and block the other. These are access and licensing decisions, not a new content-optimization method. [7]

Internal links help crawlers find pages and show how topics connect. That is standard technical SEO. A clear site structure is more useful than dozens of thin pages written for tiny variations of the same prompt. Query fan-out means a system may search several related questions; it does not mean every wording needs its own URL.

Page speed matters for users and overall site quality, but it is not a proven shortcut to AI citations. Chen and Koudas did not find that speed explained citation differences between pages on the same site. Performance work still makes sense for user experience and SEO. That study does not support claims of a direct AI citation boost. [3]

GEO tactics that have not been proven

There is a difference between useful SEO, changes that address how retrieval works, and claims that have little evidence behind them. The tactics below may have a use in some cases, but current research does not show that they reliably increase AI visibility:

  • Adding an llms.txt file will make a site appear more often in AI answers. The sources reviewed here do not show that it changes retrieval or citation rates.
  • Every page needs an FAQ section. FAQs are useful when people have real questions that the page should answer. Adding the same templated block everywhere has no proven citation benefit. Chen and Koudas found that FAQ blocks did not explain citation differences between pages on the same site. [3]
  • More schema means more citations. Structured data should describe real page content and support search features that use it. It does not create relevance or authority, and the research does not show that adding more markup causes more AI citations. [3][4][16]
  • Short paragraphs automatically improve AI visibility. Clear, self-contained passages can help readers and systems understand a page. Paragraph length on its own is not an established ranking or citation factor across platforms.
  • Every competitor or prompt variation needs its own page. This often creates large amounts of repetitive content. A page should cover the topic and meet the user’s need. Small changes in wording do not automatically call for another URL.
  • A vendor’s share-of-voice score proves business impact. It measures whatever that vendor has defined as visibility. It does not measure visits, leads or revenue. The data and method behind the score matter more than the name of the metric.

Giving ordinary content and technical work a new name does not create a new ranking factor. Any GEO recommendation should explain what part of discovery or retrieval it is meant to improve, what evidence supports it and how the result will be measured. The same test should apply to every SEO recommendation, including the long-standing SEO myths that survive on proxies and correlation.

AI citations do not guarantee visits or revenue

Pew Research analysed 68,879 Google searches by 900 US adults in March 2025. People clicked a traditional search result in 8% of visits when an AI summary appeared, compared with 15% when there was no summary. Only 1% of visits with an AI summary included a click on one of its cited links. These figures describe that study, not every query or market. [8]

Figure 4. Share of visits that included a click on a traditional result, with and without an AI summary, in Pew Research Center’s 2025 browsing study.

Agarwal and Sen used a Chrome extension to randomly assign users to Google Search with or without AI Overviews. When an AI Overview appeared, their field experiment found 39.8% fewer outbound organic clicks and a 34.5% increase in zero-click searches. This is causal evidence within their experiment, but the results should not be treated as a universal estimate for every query, market or publisher. [11]

Khosravi and Yoganarasimhan studied the staggered rollout of AI Overviews and compared traffic to English Wikipedia with traffic to its German and French editions. Their October 25, 2026 revision reports relative declines of 5.45% and 4.82% in English Wikipedia’s external search traffic. This is one publisher and one research design, not a forecast for every website. [12]

Chartbeat reports that Google Search pageviews across its publisher network fell 34% between December 2024 and December 2025. ChatGPT referrals grew by more than 200% over the same period, but AI chatbots still accounted for less than 1% of total pageviews in the network. Similarweb estimates 770.7 million AI referral visits a month worldwide between June 2025 and May 2026, up 117.4% year over year. AI referrals are growing quickly, but these figures do not show that they have replaced organic search at scale. [13][18]

Seer Interactive studied 53 brands, 5.47 million queries and 2.43 billion organic impressions. For queries where an AI Overview appeared, organic CTR rose from 1.31% in December 2025 to 2.36% in February 2026. For queries without an AI Overview, CTR rose from 3.16% to 3.82%. The gap narrowed but remained. The study is observational, and its sample and classification method limit how widely the results can be applied. [14]

Figure 5. Organic click-through rate for queries with and without AI Overviews in Seer Interactive’s 53-brand study. CTR for queries with an AIO rose from 1.31% to 2.36%; CTR without an AIO rose from 3.16% in December 2025 to 3.82% in February 2026.

The business impact depends on the query, industry, platform, brand and type of citation. An informational publisher faces different risks from an online store targeting purchase-ready searches. No single benchmark predicts traffic changes for every site, and a citation count alone does not show whether the business gained anything.

Useful business measures include AI referral visits, landing pages, engagement, leads and conversions. Track linked citations and unlinked mentions separately. Then compare referral value with other channels. If referrer data is missing or attribution is incomplete, report that gap instead of presenting estimates as measured results.

SparkToro and Datos estimated that 58.5% of US Google searches and 59.7% of EU Google searches in 2024 ended without a click to the open web. This gives useful context for zero-click search before AI summaries became widespread. It does not explain current traffic losses or isolate the effect of AI summaries. [15]

Measuring AI visibility as part of SEO

AI visibility is not a single metric. It covers several different outcomes: whether a brand appears in an answer, whether a particular page is cited, how prominently the source is presented, and whether that visibility produces visits or business results. Treating all of these as one score obscures what is actually happening.

Reliable measurement starts with repeatability. AI answers can change between runs, even when the query stays the same. Small wording changes can also produce different sources and citations. The SIGIR study found that AI Overviews were less consistent and more sensitive to small query changes than traditional search results. A single screenshot therefore says little about sustained visibility. [17]

The query set matters as much as the measurement itself. A useful sample reflects the searches that matter to the business: informational, commercial and transactional queries, branded searches, non-branded searches and competitor comparisons. Results also depend on the platform, location, search mode and whether web search is enabled. Without consistent conditions, comparisons over time become difficult to interpret.

A citation is more informative when its context is preserved. The exact URL, the claim it supports and its role in the answer matter more than a domain-level mention alone. A brand can appear as a source, a recommendation, a passing reference or a negative example. These are different forms of visibility and should not be treated as equivalent.

The platforms also need to remain distinct. ChatGPT, AI Overviews, Gemini and Perplexity do not consistently retrieve or cite the same sources. A combined visibility score can hide substantial differences between them, particularly when the underlying methodology does not explain how repeated answers, citations and mentions are counted. [9][10][17]

Traffic measurement introduces another distinction. Server logs show crawler activity, while analytics show referral sessions and what visitors do after arriving. Neither provides a complete picture on its own. Some AI referrals are not attributed correctly, and crawler activity does not establish that a page was cited or used in an answer.

The business value sits further down the measurement chain. Citation share indicates visibility; referral sessions indicate visits; engagement, leads and revenue indicate whether those visits produce value. A brand can gain citations without gaining traffic, and traffic can increase without producing meaningful business results. Combining these outcomes into one score makes the differences harder to see.

These measurements belong alongside conventional SEO reporting, not in place of it. Organic rankings, impressions, clicks, crawl activity and conversions remain relevant. AI citations and referrals add another view of search visibility, but they do not change what those existing metrics measure.

Conclusion

GEO is neither a new discipline nor a major overhaul of SEO. It is SEO adapting to search systems that retrieve passages, combine sources and generate answers. Most of the work remains familiar: making websites accessible, content relevant and useful, information clear and verifiable, and brands credible beyond their own websites. The additional work is understanding how each platform finds and cites sources, then measuring that visibility separately from visits and revenue. AI has made these services easier to market as a new discipline, but most of the techniques are not new. They are good SEO applied to a different search environment.
